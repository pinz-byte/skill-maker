# Business Innovation

Find the problem behind the symptom, learn from people doing the work, and
propose an executable change. Adapted from Rosalind Brewer's MasterClass guide.
Scale the output to the request and use the user's language.

## How to work

1. **Listen at the point of work.** Use supplied customer feedback, employee
   observations and operating data. Identify who experiences the friction and
   who has not been heard. Propose focused questions or observations where
   evidence is missing; do not invent interviews or assume executives have
   the complete view.
2. **Write the problem statement.** Specify current state, desired outcome,
   affected people, scope and measurable gap, separating facts from hypotheses.
   Trace recurring symptoms to candidate systemic causes and ask what evidence
   could distinguish them. Do not embed a favored product as the problem.
3. **Explore an executable combination.** Ask what should be preserved and
   what new capability could be added. An "and" option must meet capacity,
   cost and customer-experience constraints. Show which tasks can stop, change
   or be supported so new responsibilities do not simply overload employees.
   Compare a simpler alternative; do not treat every tradeoff as avoidable.
4. **Design the team around the problem.** Propose complementary operating,
   customer and technical perspectives, clear decision authority and one
   accountable pilot owner. Keep membership suited to the work and include
   affected voices. Agility means changing with learning, not just moving fast
   or bypassing necessary controls. Proposed staffing is not actual delegation.
5. **Run a bounded pilot plan.** State hypothesis, scope, resources, duration,
   baseline, outcome measure and guardrails for service quality and workload.
   Define what evidence would justify expanding, adjusting or stopping.
   Include execution dependencies and estimated costs with their assumptions.
6. **Review and revise.** Ask whether results support the original explanation,
   whether benefits reached the intended people and whether burdens shifted
   elsewhere. Revise when evidence changes. Protect useful capabilities that
   headline metrics may miss, while testing their claimed value.

## Delivery

Give the problem statement, recommended change, ownership and pilot criteria,
then the next decision. Use a compact comparison when alternatives matter.
For leadership-only requests, focus on a listening plan and actions to remove
the team's barriers. For a time-management issue, identify decisions the leader
must own, work to entrust to others, and a regular reflection window; do not
turn a strategy question into an unsolicited productivity program.

## Edge cases

- Missing metrics: name the observation or baseline to gather before claiming
  an improvement. A single anecdote can identify a question, not its prevalence.
- Inclusion: seek relevant perspectives and equitable access to resources;
  do not infer competence or working style from demographic categories.
- Transformation: preserve the valuable human interaction only if it actually
  matters to the users concerned; the source's historical retail examples are
  not proof that a hybrid model is right in every industry.
- A pilot plan does not authorize organizational changes, hiring or spending.

## Principles

- Listen to learn and act, not to perform consultation.
- Match the data to the problem; distinguish symptoms from causes.
- New capability needs resources, ownership and evidence of value.
