# Character Driven Fiction

Learn who a character is by putting them in a situation that matters to them.
Adapted from Judy Blume's MasterClass guide, not a formula for imitating her
voice. Work in the user's language and the story's chosen register.

## How to work

1. **Find the emotional seed.** Use an image, observation, memory or supplied
   character. Identify what they want, fear or misunderstand and what changes
   their ordinary day. A fiction seed may depart from real experience; do not
   turn it into a claim about the user or a real person.
2. **Discover through voice.** Write a short interior passage, unsent letter
   or scene in which the character wants something. Choose concrete reactions
   rather than a list of adjectives. Let the attempt reveal a contradiction,
   relationship or detail the initial biography missed. Keep a brief continuity
   note only when it will support subsequent work.
3. **Respect the viewpoint.** A child or adolescent can have complicated
   feelings and imperfect understanding. Use what this particular character
   notices and knows; avoid an adult explaining the lesson through them.
   Match language to the individual and setting rather than a stereotype of age.
4. **Let dialogue change something.** Identify what each speaker wants and
   what they avoid saying. Use interruptions, choices of words and selective
   silence to reveal character and move the scene. Dialogue need not be
   grammatically formal, but it should be readable and attributable. Avoid
   decorative tags or fashionable slang that does no work.
5. **Grow the plot from consequences.** Give the character an obstacle tied
   to their world and a choice with consequences. Write the next scene that
   changes knowledge, relationships, options or stakes. Scenes can be discovered
   out of order; arrange them so their consequences connect. If stuck, return
   to what the character values, not an arbitrary twist.
6. **Revise without flattening.** First address scene purpose and emotional
   credibility, then pacing, dialogue and sentences. Give readers room to
   interpret the situation. Cut an explicit lesson when the action already
   conveys it. Evaluate feedback against the intended effect rather than treating
   one reader's taste as a universal rule.

## Delivery

If asked to write, produce the scene, dialogue or character passage rather than
only advice. If asked to diagnose a draft, cite the actual moment where voice,
agency or emotional continuity fails and offer a revised passage. Keep notes
brief and preserve the author's existing premise unless a change is requested.
For an exercise, choose one discovery task instead of a full character survey.

## Edge cases

- A detailed character biography cannot replace action on the page. Use one
  relevant detail in a scene before expanding the dossier.
- Sensitive material should illuminate the character or story, not appear as
  gratuitous provocation. Do not assume a young viewpoint requires either
  sanitizing all conflict or making it graphic.
- Observe and research responsibly. Do not encourage covert access to children,
  private conversations or places requiring permission.
- Drafting and revision do not authorize submitting manuscripts or contacting
  editors. Do not repeat historical publishing advice as current requirements.

## Principles

- Specific wants, reactions and choices make the character believable.
- Let young characters experience complexity without an adult sermon.
- Discovery and revision can coexist; no single drafting sequence fits everyone.
