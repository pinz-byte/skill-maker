# Persuasive Selling

Help the audience make an informed choice by connecting a real problem to a
credible benefit and a manageable next step. Adapted from Daniel Pink's
MasterClass guide; use the user's language, market and established brand voice.

## How to work

1. **Define the change.** Identify the audience, offer, desired action, available
   proof and constraints. Ask whether accepting would actually benefit them.
   If it would not, revise the offer or recommendation instead of strengthening
   pressure. Separate known customer evidence from assumed motivations.
2. **Attune.** Explore the customer's priorities, alternatives and decision
   process. Ask a problem-finding question before pitching the product. Consider
   their perspective without claiming to know their mind. Authority over someone
   makes checking their genuine willingness more important, not less.
3. **Clarify the problem.** Separate the requested product from the outcome
   sought and possible root causes. Curate the information needed to compare
   credible options. A simpler intervention or no purchase may be appropriate.
   Do not manufacture a problem or omit a material drawback to create demand.
4. **Frame the value.** Select a frame that fits the evidence: the experience
   enabled, the potential supported by a mechanism, or a real loss avoided.
   Potential is not demonstrated performance; a loss is not a license to invent
   urgency. Use social proof only when sourced and relevant to this buyer.
5. **Invite collaboration.** Write a short pitch with the problem, credible
   benefit, proof or mechanism, and a clear invitation to respond. A question
   pitch can help the customer articulate their own reasons; avoid a question
   whose only function is to corner them. Rhyme is optional and must not weaken
   clarity or credibility. End with a feasible next step, not a forced close.
6. **Adapt and learn.** Listen to objections and update the case. Acknowledge
   the valid concern before adding information; "yes, and" is not automatic
   agreement with a false premise. Treat rejection as a particular outcome:
   distinguish offer fit, timing and delivery from global claims about the
   seller's identity. Respect refusal and opt-outs.

## Timing and preparation

Use interrogative preparation: what would make this credible, what could fail,
and how will the user respond? A short premortem is more useful than empty
confidence. Choose timing using the audience's availability and actual patterns;
do not assume everyone peaks in the morning or that a particular weekday
guarantees success. Adapt to fatigue and time pressure rather than exploiting them.

## Delivery

Provide the customer problem, selected frame and pitch, then the most useful
discovery question, anticipated objection and next step. Flag unsupported claims
that need evidence. For a revision request, improve the supplied pitch directly.
For a debrief, focus on what the response reveals and one change to test.

## Edge cases

- A customer already well informed needs synthesis and an honest comparison,
  not a beginner lecture or assumed information superiority.
- An untested pitch is a candidate. Do not predict a conversion rate or claim
  that a psychological technique makes someone buy.
- Do not invent testimonials, competitor weaknesses, urgency or customer research.
  Use questions to understand a person, not exploit vulnerabilities.
- Drafting does not authorize sending, enrolling, purchasing or committing.

## Principles

- Make the customer's problem clearer before making the offer more persuasive.
- Separate relevance, evidence and framing; fluency does not prove truth.
- A useful next step can be further discovery or an honest no.
