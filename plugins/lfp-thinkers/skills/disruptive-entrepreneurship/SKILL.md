# Disruptive Entrepreneurship

Find an underserved experience, propose a better one and learn without treating
recklessness as courage. Adapted from Richard Branson's MasterClass guide.
Use the user's language and distinguish an opportunity from an approved launch.

## How to work

1. **Start with productive dissatisfaction.** Identify a specific frustrating
   experience, affected customer and existing workaround. Distinguish the
   founder's irritation from evidence that others share it or would pay to fix it.
   Ask what an incumbent convention protects before deciding it is pointless.
2. **Define the meaningful difference.** State the customer promise and the
   mechanism that makes it possible. Compare it to the actual alternative,
   including doing nothing. A flashy feature, famous founder or lower price
   alone is not an operating advantage. Mark missing market evidence explicitly.
3. **Make risk deliberate.** Name assumptions about demand, delivery, cost,
   permissions and capacity. Separate reversible experiments from commitments
   that could threaten the user's resources or other people. Define a maximum
   affordable loss of money, time and reputation using the user's actual limits.
   Do not derive a risk budget from inspirational anecdotes.
4. **Test the opportunity.** Choose the smallest credible demonstration of the
   promised experience. Specify who would try it, what they would do, what
   observation counts as evidence and what would lead to changing or stopping.
   Interest, a trial, repeat use and paid demand are different signals. If costs
   are material, sketch unit economics with visible assumptions and sensitivity.
5. **Build an accountable team plan.** Identify capabilities, an owner, decision
   rights, resources and checkpoints. Entrust outcomes rather than prescribe
   every step. Keep accountability for the work and credit for contributors.
   Proposed delegation does not authorize hiring, contacting people or agents.
6. **Capture and act on learning.** Record observations and decisions in a
   concise experiment log if the user wants an artifact. On a setback, identify
   what failed and what still holds before deciding to persist, pivot or stop.
   Do not frame every failure as a reason to double down.

## Include impact in the design

When relevant, examine effects on the team, customers, community and wider
environment. Turn a purpose claim into a concrete operating commitment and a
way to observe it. Do not substitute publicity, a donation claim or a label for
evidence of impact. Accommodate people's actual strengths and access needs
without inferring talent from a diagnosis or personality stereotype.

## Delivery

Provide the frustration, proposed difference, biggest unresolved assumption,
bounded test, responsible role and go/change/stop criteria. For an existing
idea, focus on its weakest assumption. For a delegation request, give an outcome
brief and check-in plan instead of a whole venture strategy.

## Edge cases

- Expensive or regulated ventures: identify the validation and qualified input
  needed before commitments. Do not bypass requirements in the name of disruption.
- No customer access: propose a way to obtain evidence; do not report imagined
  interviews or traction as completed work.
- A survivorship anecdote illustrates a move, not its probability of success.
  Historical Virgin examples do not establish current opportunities.
- Drafting and planning do not authorize spending, launching or fundraising.

## Principles

- Test a customer benefit, not the founder's appetite for risk.
- Give people autonomy with resources, clear outcomes and accountability.
- Use failure as information while preserving the ability to try again.
