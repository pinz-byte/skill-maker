# Scientific Problem Solving

Use observation, study, hypothesis, test, comparison and reporting to learn
what an explanation can withstand. Adapted from Bill Nye's MasterClass guide.
Scale the investigation to the question and use the user's language.

## How to work

1. **Observe precisely.** Describe what occurred, when, under which conditions
   and how it was measured. Separate an observation from an explanation or a
   proposed remedy. Define the outcome the user wants to understand or improve.
2. **Study what is already known.** Check identifiable, relevant sources and
   their methods, dates and incentives. Trace repeated claims to their original
   evidence. Marketing or a conflict of interest warrants scrutiny, not automatic
   dismissal. Personal preference and repetition are not corroboration.
3. **State competing hypotheses.** Make a specific explanation and a prediction
   about what should be observed if it holds. Include at least one credible
   alternative where the evidence permits one. State what result would count
   against the explanation. Simplicity is a comparison aid, not proof.
4. **Design an informative, safe test.** Identify the changed factor, measured
   outcome, baseline or comparison, conditions to hold constant and potential
   confounders. Specify units, observation period and a decision criterion
   before looking at results. Use repeated observations or controlled order
   where variation could mislead. Choose a design proportional to the claim.
5. **Perform only what can actually be done.** Keep a test proposal separate
   from execution. Record actual conditions and anomalies when evidence is
   available. Do not invent measurements, repeat counts or statistical certainty.
   A failed instrument or implementation can make a result inconclusive.
6. **Compare and report.** Compare the prediction to the observations and their
   uncertainty, considering alternatives. Report what is supported, contradicted
   or still unresolved, the limits of the test and the next useful revision.
   An intervention that helps does not by itself prove the proposed mechanism.

## Evaluate an existing claim

For a critique request, start with the claim and inspect specificity, source,
measurement, comparison and independent checkability. Ask what the claimant
would accept as contrary evidence. Distinguish a weak argument from a false
claim, and lack of evidence from demonstrated absence. Do not present two
positions as equally supported merely because both are expressed confidently.

## Delivery

Return the question, hypothesis and predicted observation, then the test or
evidence assessment and a conclusion matched to what was actually checked.
When no test has run, deliver a ready-to-use test plan and data-recording fields,
not simulated results. Use a small table if it improves comparisons.

## Edge cases

- High variation or small samples: describe uncertainty and avoid universal
  conclusions from one success. Use statistics only when warranted by the design.
- Experiments involving health, hazardous materials, electricity or other
  meaningful risks require suitable expertise and safeguards. Prefer an existing
  credible dataset or a safe demonstration over an improvised risky test.
- For current science or environmental quantities, verify primary sources and
  scope. Old guide statistics are not present-day measurements.
- A correlation or an anecdotal before/after comparison is not a controlled
  experiment. State what its design can actually establish.

## Principles

- Make the explanation vulnerable to a meaningful check.
- Distinguish a useful intervention from proof of its mechanism.
- Report failed and inconclusive tests as honestly as successful ones.
